classification using VGG19-based 
feature encoding
Fallah H. Najjar1,2, Zaid Nidhal Khudhair1,3, Farhan Mohamed1,4,  
Mohd Shafry Mohd Rahim1,4, Vei Siang Chan1,4 & Ali Hilal Ali5
Skin cancer is among the most widely distributed, deadliest cancers around the globe, and early 
diagnosis becomes vital to enhance patient survival. Deep learning has demonstrated high potential 
for automatic skin lesion classification. However, existing Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are 
still unsatisfactory in terms of dataset reliance, subjection to orientations, and the inability to model 
long-range global context. To solve this problem, we propose a hybrid model named the VGG19-
RSPDA-ViT with the fine-grained local feature captured by VGG19 and the global context provided by 
Vision Transformers (ViT). The proposed RSPDA enforces rotation invariance and enriches the feature 
space, which further strengthens generalization on a small training set. To the best of our knowledge, 
this is the first work to systematically combine feature-map-level rotation/shift augmentation with 
a CNN-Transformer hybrid model for dermoscopic skin cancer detection. Performance was validated 
on three benchmark datasets: the Melanoma Skin Cancer Dataset of 10,000 Images (MSK10000) for 
binary classification and the Human Against Machine with 10,000 training images (HAM10000) and 
Hospital Pedro Hispano (PH2) for multi-class classification. Our model achieved accuracies of 97.9%, 
97.1%, and 98.67% on MSK10000, HAM10000, and PH2 datasets, respectively , with consistently high 
macro-averaged precision, recall, specificity, and F1 scores across both datasets. VGG19-RSPDA-ViT 
outperformed existing state-of-the-art methods with superior generalization capabilities. These results 
demonstrate that our proposed model is effective for skin lesion classification and has significant 
potential for clinical application as an automated diagnostic tool in dermatology .
Keywords Skin cancer, VGG19-RSPDA-ViT, Image augmentation, Skin lesion classification
Skin cancer is a major global health challenge and is one of the most common cancers worldwide1. The World 
Health Organization (WHO) reports that millions are diagnosed every year, with melanoma responsible for 
a significant number of skin cancer deaths2. Melanoma is an especially aggressive type of skin cancer that 
can quickly spread to other parts of the patient’s body if it is not detected early3. Diagnosis of this syndrome 
depends on dermoscopic imaging, but a large variation in the shape, color and texture of the lesion makes expert 
evaluation difficult4,5. Deep learning techniques, notably Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) like VGG, 
ResNet, and Inception, achieve the state-of-the-art performance on benchmark datasets like the International 
Skin Imaging Collaboration (ISIC), Melanoma Skin Cancer Dataset of 10,000 Images (MSK10000), the Human 
Against Machine with 10,000 training images (HAM10000), and Hospital Pedro Hispano (PH2). These models 
perform well for local texture and edge patterns, reaching classification accuracy that is nearly comparable to 
expert dermatologists6. Recently, a Vision Transformer (ViT) has been proposed for medical image analysis, 
including dermoscopy and other skin imaging tasks, utilizing a global self-attention mechanism for learning the 
long dependencies among patches. There has also been promising work on hybrid CNN-Transformer models 
(e.g., TransMed, Swin-UNet), mostly in the segmentation and multimodal settings.
Nevertheless, Dermoscopic images, providing a detailed view of the skin surface micropatterns, are often 
used to diagnose skin lesions7. Furthermore, dermoscopic analysis remains challenging because skin lesions

exhibit considerable morphological variation8,9, such as shape, size, color, and texture. For example, nevi (moles) 
belong to benign lesions but can resemble early-stage melanomas, complicating the interpretability10. Other 
variables include skin tone, lighting conditions, and the anatomical location of the lesion. These variations 
necessitate more accurate and automated skin lesion classification systems to aid dermatologists in decision-
making. Recently, deep learning has become a state-of-the-art method to automate skin cancer diagnosis with 
accuracy comparable to that of trained dermatologists in image classification tasks11.
CNNs make an impressive contribution to medical imaging based on their hierarchical level depth of input 
image learning, allowing deep representation of the learning approach12,13. CNNs can learn how to recognize 
each type of skin lesion automatically from low-level features, such as local edges, textures, and patterns14–17. 
Models like ResNet, Inception and VGGs have been widely applied in skin lesion classification and achieved state-
of-the-art results on benchmark datasets, such as the International Skin Imaging Collaboration (ISIC) archive18. 
One of the most adopted networks for feature extraction is VGG19, proposed by the Visual Geometry Group at 
the University of Oxford, which is simple and powerful19. VGG19 has 19 layers, of which 16 are convolutional, 
and three are fully connected, making it ideal for extracting fine-grained features from dermoscopic images. Its 
consistent design with a small 3×3 convolutional filter size enables deeper levels of feature learning, which is 
useful in image characteristics measurement20.
Nevertheless, although their filters can extract important visual structures and fine-grained local features, 
VGG19 and other CNNs fundamentally lack global context due to their localized convolution filters. Adding 
more layers in CNNs can partially resolve this constraint by extending the receptive field21. However, this 
approach also risks overfitting and increased computational cost, particularly when the datasets are small or 
highly variable22.
In order to overcome the limitations of CNNs, ViT has been proposed as a new paradigm for image 
classification23. Initially motivated by the advances of transformers in Natural Language Processing (NLP), ViT 
demonstrated that self-attention mechanisms can be successfully transferred to the image analysis domain24. 
In contrast to CNNs, ViT passes a convolutional kernel over the input image slidingly and treats an image as a 
sequence of patches. Fixed-size patches are used to partition each image, which is then flattened and linearly 
embedded into tokens for the transformer to process using multi-head self-attention25. ViT’s global attention 
mechanism enables it to learn long-range dependencies between patches, capturing information on the entire 
image context, which is often tougher for CNNs to learn. For diverse medical images, in which the spatial 
relationships between regions are critical, ViT can effectively master intricate relationships among regions 
throughout the whole image. Despite its advantage, applying ViT directly to medical image analysis remains a 
challenge26.
One of the major limitations is their large data requirement: ViT often requires large-scale datasets to learn 
better representations. Medical imaging datasets, such as skin lesions, are generally small since acquiring and 
annotating high-quality medical images is meticulous27. Moreover, ViT can be more sensitive to image orientation 
and scale since they do not have the inductive bias that makes CNNs robust to such variances28. This sensitivity 
can be especially problematic when dealing with skin lesions, which can be inspected in multiple orientations, 
i.e., the same lesion can be imaged in many ways when looking at either arm or the scale. Moreover, delicate 
skin image details might get lost due to differences in distance or angle used to capture the image. Consequently, 
the performance of a ViT-based method is highly contingent on the augmentation and preprocessing methods 
employed29.
Data augmentation is a common technique for enhancing model generalization by introducing variation in 
training data. Augmentations, such as rotations, flips, color adjustments, and translations, have been used in skin 
lesion classification to make the model more robust to variations30. On the other hand, simple augmentation fails 
to derive spatial correlation between different lesion areas. For instance, a melanoma lesion may have wavering 
edges and a color variegation pattern that must be scanned in multiple directions to ascertain that the model 
captures the subtle malignancy features31. Flexibility in dimensions becomes an obstacle in the localization 
task as the spatial aspect is still a key point, and the scarcity of data presents a difficulty for both CNNs and 
transformers.
To mitigate these problems, we have developed a compound model for skin lesion classification, denoted 
as VGG19-RSPDA-ViT. First, a pretrained VGG19 is used as a backbone to capture fine-grained dermoscopic 
features. Then, our new RSPDA can produce orientation-invariant feature-map views in the second-hand 
stage to increase spatial diversity. These augmented features are tokenized and fed into a light ViT encoder that 
learns contextual relationships among lesion areas. Finally, a fully connected classifier heads that predict lesion 
category in both binary and multi-class. This model combines the strengths of CNNs and Transformers while 
addressing their weaknesses.
Our main contributions are: (i) introducing Rotated and Shifted Patch Data Augmentation Vision Transformer 
(VGG19-RSPDA-ViT), a novel hybrid model that combines VGG19 and ViT to enhance the accuracy of skin 
lesion classification. (ii) Integrate the well-established VGG19 architecture to extract rich local features that 
capture the fine-grained textural details of skin lesions. (iii) Incorporate the RSPDA tokenization strategy 
within the ViT, which enables the processing of augmented feature maps through rotation and shift operations 
that enhance training data diversity and introduce rotational invariance. Finally, significant improvements in 
skin lesion classification accuracy were demonstrated through: (iv) Minimizing overfitting while maintaining 
consistency across different benchmark datasets, and (v) effectively addressing orientation sensitivity and the 
limited availability of annotated data.
Although few hybrid CNN-Transformer architectures have been applied in medical imaging (e.g., TransMed, 
MedT, Swin-UNet), they are mostly focused on segmentation tasks or multimodal analysis, which involves 
working with more than one type of information and is dependent on complex hierarchical transformer 
structures. Thus, our work focuses on dermoscopic skin lesion classification, addressing two main challenges: (i)

Related works
In the evolving field of skin cancer diagnosis, researchers have developed various methods, including deep 
learning and machine learning approaches, to improve the accuracy and speed of melanoma detection. The 
innovation process begins with the work of Subhayu Ghosh et al.32, who proposed an ensemble model to combine 
the advantages of DCNNs, Capsule Networks (Caps-Net), and ViT for the process of feature extraction. These 
extracted features were used as input to classical classifiers (Random Forest, SVM, XGBoost), and an ensemble 
model, achieving 91.6% accuracy on the MSK10000 dataset. However, while the model relies on handcrafted 
ensemble mechanisms that are difficult to interpret, this dependency may limit real-time adaptability in clinical 
applications. Focusing on multimodal learning, Adebiyi et al.33 filled the gap for such a context by introducing 
dermoscopic images and patient metadata as a multimodal input to a deep learning model. Tested on 
HAM10000 and ISIC2017 datasets, their model achieved superior performance compared to unimodal models 
with an accuracy of 94.11% and an AUC of 0.9426 on HAM10000. Although this method offers valuable real-life 
applicability, incomplete clinical information may limit its effectiveness in resource-constrained settings.
Furthermore, Ingle and Shaikh compared the performance of plain CNNs with VGG16 on HAM10000 
data. The authors found that VGG16 performed better with the use of data augmentation and regularization, 
achieving an accuracy of 88.83%. This study revealed the strength of transfer learning and increased network 
depth for multi-class categorization problems, although class imbalance and inter-class overlap continue to 
pose difficulties34. Similarly, Wicaksana et al. investigated the single-stage detection model YOLOv11 with the 
HAM10000 dataset. YOLOv11x-cls attained 84.74% accuracy, while VGG19 achieved 89.68%, demonstrating 
that multi-stage architectures are superior in fine-grained classification. Their results also highlighted the effect 
of data augmentation in addressing class imbalance and revealed the limitations of single-stage networks35.
In contrast, Sinchana et al.36 proposed a hybrid CNN model with ResNet and VGG backbones using transfer 
learning on the MSK10000 training dataset. They investigated the choice of optimizer (RMSProp, Adam or 
SGD) and the performance measure (specificity, sensitivity). Although the model showed robust performance, 
its generalizability remained uncertain, requiring validation on external cohort datasets. Furthermore, Sabir and 
Mehmood37 conducted a comparative study of CNN, ResNet-18, and EfficientNet-B0. EfficientNet-B0 appeared 
to be the most promising and performed with an accuracy of 97%, a precision of 95%, and a sensitivity of 99%. 
This work confirmed the potential of the model and also observed its sensitivity to hyperparameter settings 
and quality of preprocessing with rigorous metric analysis. Al Huda et al.38 developed DeepMelaNet, designed 
for early-stage melanoma detection. This model, trained on the MSK10000 dataset, achieved 93.40% accuracy. 
Their novel deep-learning architecture achieved balanced F1-scores across all classes. However, the study also 
reported several difficulties in stage-specific categorization and dataset diversity.
Bhuvaneswari and Raman evaluated AlexNet, and it achieved only 91.1% compared with InceptionV3 and 
VGG19. Although this work demonstrated the continuing utility of AlexNet in medical imaging, which represents 
lesion complexity due to a small field of view and retained features, its relatively shallow depth and hard feature 
extraction hamper its ability to address complex lesion patterns39. Srivastava et al.40 made significant advances 
in ensemble learning through VCCINet, a multi-model ensemble combining CNN, CapsNet, InceptionV3 and 
feature-extracted VGG16. Their refined model, FT-VCCINet, integrated pre-trained knowledge and achieved 
better performance than the single model. The ensemble model delivered consistent but marginal gains across 
precision, recall, F1-score, and specificity, although the performance fluctuations across training epochs revealed 
the model’s sensitivity to training schedules.
To balance local texture encoding and global context modeling in dermoscopic image analysis, recent studies 
have progressively integrated convolutional and transformer paradigms. Aruk et al.41 combined a ConvNeXt-
CAFormer hybrid, whereby ConvNeXt blocks extract fine-grained local patterns and transformer stages capture 
long-range dependencies. Under identical training conditions to strong CNN and ViT baselines on HAM10000 
and ISIC 2019, the authors report that this hybrid achieves 94.30% accuracy and 91.11% F1 on HAM10000, and 
92.50% accuracy and 90.38% F1 on ISIC 2019, outperforming more than twenty competitors.
In contrast, Y ang et al.42 proposed the ViTfSCD pipeline, a ViT-based architecture customized for dermoscopy 
comprising four phases: data augmentation and class rebalancing, patch tokenization, transformer encoder, and 
batch normalization regularized classifier. They attained 94.1% accuracy with HAM10000, demonstrating that 
oversampling/undersampling combined with duplicate removal are critical. They also highlighted ViT’s data-
efficiency limitations, which often demand large-scale pre-training and illustrate open issues in robustness as 
well as generalization beyond curated dermoscopy. All together, these results establish class rebalancing and 
duplication control among the first-order considerations for deploying a transformer in dermatology.
Besides CNN-based methods, several hybrid CNN-Transformer architectural designs have been recently 
introduced in the field of medical imaging. There had already been some successful examples demonstrating 
the advantages of integrating CNNs with Transformers for multimodal learning (e.g., TransMed, MedT) and 
medical image segmentation tasks. At the same time, hierarchical self-attention has also successfully been applied 
to medical images through Swin-UNet variants. While useful in their own right, these techniques are largely 
targeted either for segmentation or multimodal tasks and rely on deeper hierarchical architectures. In contrast, 
our skin lesion classification for dermoscopy does not require ImageNet pre-training, uses a low-resource and 
interpretable design (incorporating VGG19 feature extraction into the proposed lightweight Vision Transformer 
powered with RSPDA), demonstrating promising results on both robustness and generalization.

Lastly, Waheed et al.7 introduced a CNN-based architecture designed for melanoma diagnosing. They 
attempted to solve the problem by preprocessing and augmenting the noisy and various image inputs. The 
approach yielded high diagnosis accuracy and was promising for low-resource implementation. However, its 
internal validation introduces the potential for overfitting and external generalizability.
Further, previous works have shown significant results in skin lesion classification, but they still have 
limitations. Ensemble-based methods, such as those by Ghosh et al.32, achieve high accuracy but face challenges 
in interpretability and increased computational burden, making them unsuitable for clinical deployment. 
Multimodal approaches by Adebiyi et al.33 demonstrated the importance of clinical metadata. Nevertheless, 
the requirement for complete clinical data, coupled with patient privacy considerations, may limit practical 
applicability in resource-constrained settings34.
Wicaksana et al.35 showed that deeper architectures like InceptionV3 and GoogLeNet achieve superior 
performance by capturing fine-grained lesion texture patterns; these models remain biased due to class imbalance 
and have difficulty generalizing to underrepresented lesion types. Regarding transfer learning approaches, 
studies by Sinchana et al.36 and Sabir and Mehmood37 showed high recall, but their generalizability on external 
validation datasets remains unknown.
Recent works have presented some results on PH2. For example, Ghazouani43 proposed the Multi-Residual 
Attention Network (MuRANet), which combines multi-scale dilated, spatial, channel-wise and squeeze-and-
excitation attention strategies. Their method attained 97–98% accuracy and F1-score on PH2, which was 
considered one of the best. In contrast, Golkarieh et al.44 achieved lower accuracy with 89.53% and a recall of 
90.97%, as well as an F1-score of 90.62%, which suggests that it is not feasible to incorporate lesion variability 
properly.
In comparison to these works, our VGG19-RSPDA-ViT obtained an accuracy of 98.67% and a F1-score of 
97.72% on PH2, which is slightly superior to MuRANet. These comparisons demonstrate the competitiveness 
of attention-augmented deep learning strategies on PH2 and the effectiveness of our hybrid CNN-Transformer 
framework for modelling local and global lesion features. Finally, our developed VGG19-RSPDA-ViT method 
addresses these limitations by combining fine-grained CNN feature extraction with transformer-based 
global context modeling. The RSPDA augmentation strategy provides rotational invariance while improving 
classification performance on imbalanced datasets.
Methodology
The proposed VGG19-RSPDA-ViT model combines the strengths of CNNs and ViT to improve skin lesion 
classification. The methodology comprises three primary stages: feature extraction using the VGG19 backbone, 
rotated and shifted patch tokenization, and ViT encoding. A visual overview of this architecture is presented in 
Fig.  1. Below, we describe each stage in detail and the associated mathematical formulations.
The input image is first processed through the VGG19 network, generating feature maps. The RSPDA method 
then generates multiple rotated and shifted versions of the feature map, which are concatenated and tokenized 
into patches. These patches are fed into ViT, which applies self-attention and Feed-Forward Network (FFN) to 
learn complex spatial relationships. The final representation is passed through a dense layer for classification to 
predict the skin lesion category.
Data preprocessing and augmentation
All images are rescaled to 168 × 168 and normalized using the mean/variance calculated from the training 
set. Normalization is always used for training, validation and test pipelines. We randomly apply stochastic 
augmentations during training and validation to improve generalization and alleviate overfitting. These 
augmentations consist of random horizontal flips, small-angle random rotations up to ± 0.02 (about ± 7°) and 
random zooms of up to 20% in height and width. Figure  2 displays an example of the applied augmentations 
and demonstrates how one dermoscopic image can appear under different augmentation settings. The test set 
underwent only resizing and normalization to make the evaluation unbiased.
To prevent data leakage, we ensure that patient-level splitting is followed strictly, i.e., images belonging to 
the same patient do not occur in more than one subset. This process involves two steps: 80% of the dataset for 
training, and the remaining 20% is auxiliary data, which is further split between 10% validation and 10% test 
datasets. The process is randomized but reproducible using a fixed seed (random_state = 101). This approach is 
designed such that when performance metrics are used to evaluate model generalization, they verify the model’s 
ability to generalize to unseen patients rather than memorizing different views from the same patient.

In the second stage, the extracted feature map F(x) is processed using a Rotated and Shifted Patch Data 
Augmentation (RSPDA) strategy. This stage introduces rotational invariance and enhances the spatial context 
for the transformer. The RSPDA method includes the following steps.
Firstly, the feature map F(x) is rotated by angles θ∈(0◦,90◦,180◦,270◦), see Fig.  4. Let the feature 
map Rθ(F(x))denote the rotation procedure:
 Rθ(F(x)) = rotate((F (x)),θ) (2)
For shifting the rotated feature map Rθ(F(x)), a four-directional shift is employed with dx and dy that 
represent the x-dimension and y-dimension, respectively. We also examine how rotation and translation affect 
spatial feature uniformity. Equation 3 is used to calculate the S(dx,dy):
 S(dx,dy)(Rθ(F(x))) = shift (Rθ(F(x)),dx,dy) (3)

Results and discussion
To evaluate the efficiency and the generalizability of the proposed deep learning model to classify skin lesions, 
we performed the experiments on three publicly available dermatology datasets widely employed in the 
literature: HAM10000, MSK10000, and PH2. All collections contain dermoscopic images of skin lesions with a 
common clinical task. However, they differ in the sources of data collection, the visual content of images, and 
the population of patients. Our experiments used the standard training, validation and test splits of HAM10000, 
MSK10000, and PH2 to ensure no data overlap between partitions. Also, 5-fold validation splitting is used for 
the PH2 dataset. There was no data leakage as duplicate or near-duplicate images were not shared across splits, 
and data preprocessing steps were added independently within each portion. We also used a full set of threshold-
dependent and threshold-independent evaluation metrics to assess model performance. Accuracy, precision, 
recall, specificity, F1-score, and Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) were the main performance metrics.

Limitations and future work
A limitation is that we have not performed cross-validation or validated our results with other independent 
clinical cohorts. While our model provided strong performance on two benchmark datasets, indicating its 
generalizability, validation in larger independent clinical cohorts or via using k-fold cross-validation would 
provide more evidence of its generalizability. We see further exploration in this direction as important future work. 
Finally, it is crucial to mention that the nature of the HAM10000 dataset is imbalanced by default, and certain 
types of lesions are underrepresented. Most classes maintained a precision-recall balance with our approach, 
although minority categories were sometimes misclassified; this is due to the difficulty of distinguishing between 
rare lesion types. Although we did not use explicit balancing methods (e.g., weighted loss or focal loss) in our 
framework, applying them might help to improve classification performance for imbalanced classes in future 
work.
The VGG19-RSPDA-ViT model may be further developed for incorporation into clinical pipelines as an 
assistive decision-making system. This could also assist in daily dermoscopic workflows for dermoscopic 
diagnosis by reducing inter-observer differences, providing a rapid and reproducible second opinion of the 
dermoscopic interpretation. In teledermatology scenarios, automated triage of lesions could enable pre-filtering 
of high-risk cases for priority expert review, thus facilitating quick diagnosis in underserved areas. Outside of 
specialist use, the system may be mounted as a pre-screening module at the primary or community care level, 
where it could aid non-expert clinicians in identifying lesions for referral. Several additional steps are needed 
for safe clinical adoption, including prospective validation in a range of clinical cohorts, regulatory approval, 
and the creation of user-friendly interfaces that can be easily added to the existing electronic health record and 
imaging systems.
Ethical considerations are essential in the development and implementation of AI-based skin cancer 
diagnostic tools. While HAM10000 MSK10000, and PH2 are public benchmark datasets in which patient data 
has been de-identified, real-world medical applications must adhere to strict privacy and consent regulations 
(e.g., HIPAA, GDPR). In addition, all datasets present biases towards lighter skin tones and specific acquisition 
devices, which could exacerbate algorithmic bias against underrepresented populations. Misdiagnosis also has 
strong ethical implications, as false negatives could delay identification of malignant lesions, while false positives 
could cause unnecessary patient anxiety and treatment. We therefore emphasise that the proposed system 
should serve as a decision-support tool to dermatologists, not as a replacement for them. Prospective validation 
in diverse cohorts with various skin tones and careful clinician oversight of any such system is essential to ensure 
the system is equitable, transparent, and accountable.
Ablation study
To investigate the contribution of the proposed RSPDA, we compare two variations of our framework: (i) 
VGG19-ViT and (ii) VGG19-RSPDA-ViT. This comparison allows us to examine RSPDA ’s influence on feature 
representation and classification performance.
Model structure comparison
The VGG19-ViT model uses a VGG19 backbone and applies a basic patchification operation that extracts nine 
tokens from a 3 × 3 grid across the feature map. These tokens are processed through 4 Transformer encoder 
blocks, linearized, and fed through a two-layer MLP with 2048 and 1024 hidden units for classification. This 
creates a compact structure with about 10.7  million parameters, of which 8.4  million are trainable. Most 
parameters are concentrated in the classifier layers. The model is lightweight and efficient since the Transformer 
blocks process only nine tokens. However, using only nine tokens limits spatial diversity, and the absence of 
rotation or shift augmentations prevents learning orientation-invariant features.
In contrast, the VGG19-RSPDA-ViT variant replaces simple patchification with a tokenizer that performs 
four rotations and four pixel shifts, providing 16 different views per image. Each view generates 9 tokens, 
resulting in 144 tokens total. They are then passed into the same four-layer Transformer encoder. However, the 
quadratic complexity of self-attention significantly increases computational cost: while the baseline requires only 
81 pairwise interactions per layer, RSPDA requires over 20,000. The size of the flattened feature representation 
also grows significantly, from 2304 dimensions in the baseline to 36,864 in the RSPDA model. When flattened 
into the 2048-unit dense layer, this yields a single weight matrix with over 75  million parameters, bringing 
the total parameter count to 81.5 million, nearly eight times larger than VGG19-ViT. Consequently, VGG19-
RSPDA-ViT has significantly higher memory and computational requirements than VGG19-ViT for both 
training and inference. Table  5 presents the model complexity comparison between the VGG19-ViT and the

Comparative results
To evaluate RSPDA ’s specific contribution, we compared the baseline VGG19-ViT model with our proposed 
VGG19-RSPDA-ViT. As shown in Table  6, RSPDA improves accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, and F1-score 
on both MSK10000 and HAM10000 datasets. This demonstrates that RSPDA ’s robustness to orientation and 
translation variations significantly contributes to the model’s superior performance.
The results confirm the trade-off between efficiency and performance. The VGG19-ViT model is 
computationally lighter and faster but lacks sensitivity and suffers from inter-class confusion. VGG19-RSPDA-
ViT achieves substantial improvements in accuracy, recall, and robustness across both datasets.
Conclusions
In this study, we established a new hybrid VGG19-RSPDA-ViT model for automatic skin lesion classification 
that leverages the fine-grained local feature extraction capabilities of VGG19 and the global contextual 
learning capacity of ViT, enhanced by our proposed RSPDA scheme. Notably, unlike handcrafted image-level 
augmentations, RSPDA systematically generates rotated and shifted feature-map views before tokenization, 
enriching the sequence input to the transformer and enabling orientation-invariant representation learning. 
Through extensive experiments on the HAM10000 and MSK10000 benchmark datasets, our model achieved 
impressive performance with 97.1% and 97.9% accuracy, respectively. Ablation studies demonstrate that all 
three components (VGG19 backbone, ViT integration and RSPDA augmentation) contribute significantly, with 
RSPDA providing the most significant improvement.
Our comprehensive evaluation included ROC curves, class-wise sensitivity/specificity, and interpretability 
analyses. Grad-CAM visualizations demonstrate that the model consistently focuses on clinically relevant lesion 
regions and suppresses irrelevant background structures. These results highlight both the predictive performance 
and interpretability of our approach, which are essential for clinical adoption.

Future directions
Based on this work, a few directions for future research would be worth pursuing. First, we aim to validate 
in additional and diverse cohorts (Derm7pt, ISIC challenge datasets, as well as clinical in-house collections), 
further to substantiate the robustness and generalization of our approach. Second, we seek to investigate semi-
supervised and self-supervised learning approaches for more effectively using large unlabeled dermoscopic 
images. Third, using multimodal information, such as patient metadata, clinical images, and histopathology, 
for Desktop Routine may further improve diagnostic performance. Last but not least, optimization of the 
framework for real-time clinical application and performing a reader study with dermatologists in a prospective 
way are important intermediate steps toward clinical translation.
