Hybrid vision transformer and 
graph neural network model with 
region-adaptive attention for 
enhanced skin cancer prediction
Aswani Dogga1,4, Sivasubramanian R.2,4 & Shanthi S.3,4
A well-known and potentially lethal skin cancer requires prompt detection and diagnosis. Complex 
spatial linkages and global contextual information in skin lesion photos challenge CNNs and other 
deep learning methods. Given these restrictions, we present a Hybrid Vision Transformer (ViT) with 
a Graph Neural Network (GNN) and Region-Adaptive Attention to diagnose skin cancer. The ViT 
branch captures dermoscopy image global dependencies, whereas the GNN enhances features by 
exploiting lesions’ spatial relationships. Region-Adaptive Attention improves lesion categorization 
by dynamically improving feature extraction in diagnostically rele vant locations. Our paradigm for 
multi-scale lesion analysis accounts for lesion size, color, and texture changes. Meta-learning methods 
refine the proposed model to make it generalizable across skin tones and imaging settings. Our 
model outperformed state-of-the-art deep learning algorithms on benchmark skin cancer datasets. 
The architecture improves classification accuracy and interpretability, making it a promising clinical 
dermatology tool.
Keywords Skin cancer prediction, Vision transformer (ViT), Graph neural network (GNN), Region-
adaptive attention (RAA), Deep learning, Medical image analysis, Multi-Scale lesion analysis
Skin cancer is considered to be among the most fatal cancers, with melanoma being categorized as the most 
deadly and aggressive type. Timely intervention considerably improves survival rates; hence, early detection 
and accurate diagnosis are important for improving treatment outcome1. Clinical dermatology relies on visual 
examination of skin lesions as its mainstay of diagnosis, yet this method is subjective and prone to errors. 
In a bid to overcome some of these limitations, the automatic detection and diagnosis for skin cancer using 
deep learning-based computer-aided diagnosis techniques had gained much research attention. Recently, 
convolutional neural networks (CNN) have proved to be very effective in the field of medical image diagnosis. 
Nonetheless, CNNs suffer some challenges including small receptive fields, long-range correlation capture, and 
irregular lesion architecture. Option alternatives are the Vision Transformers (ViT), which with better global 
contextual understanding than ConvNets, achieve superior results in medical imaging2. Unfortunately, ViTs 
lack the ability to model spatial interactions among sub regions, which leads to their inability to understand the 
complex shapes of skin lesions. We thus propose a skin cancer prediction model that implements a Hybrid Vision 
Transformer (ViT) with a Graph Neural Network (GNN) incorporating Region-Adaptive Attention (RAA). The 
GNN attempts to enhance the representation of atypical skin lesions by encoding spatial interactions between 
lesion territories while the ViT exploits global dependency mapping on dermoscopic images. Our Region-
Adaptive Attention (RAA) thus increases the classification score by improving feature extraction with focus 
of attention on diagnostically salient areas3. Unlike classic approaches that rely solely on stacked convolutional 
networks or transformer-only pipelines, the proposed architecture leverages the complementary strengths of 
Vision Transformers and graph neural networks in a tightly woven, ViT-GNN hybrid design4. By deriving a 
graph from the Vision Transformer’s output maps, the framework explicitly learns the spatial and topological 
relations among lesion components, addressing the overlooked inter-region dependencies that remain absent in 
most state-of-the-art pipelines. To further sharpen the model’s focus on clinically salient lesion characteristics, a 
custom hybrid attention mechanism is employed, while complementary explainability modules—such as SHAP

Related work
Automated skin cancer classification has been rigorously explored through a combination of machine learning 
and deep learning techniques. This discussion organizes the relevant studies into three categories—CNN-based 
approaches, transformer-based frameworks, and hybrid or graph-driven models.
CNN-based skin cancer classification
CNNs remain a mainstay in skin lesion analysis, largely because they excel at fusing local spatial information. 
Pioneering work, including the study by Esteva et al.6, demonstrated dermatologist-level performance by 
applying Inception-v3 on dermoscopic images. Building on this, Kawahara et al.7 employed several ResNet 
variants, reporting consistent improvement over the ISIC benchmark. More recently, architectures such 
as EfficientNet8 and DenseNet9 have been harnessed, offering gains in predictive fidelity while keeping 
computational requirements in check. Despite these strengths, standard CNNs remain limited in their ability 
to model long-range spatial relationships, a constraint that restricts the transfer of learned knowledge across 
globally varying lesion contexts.
Transformer-based models
Transformers tend to overlook fine-grained structural nuances crucial for metastatic prognosis and the current 
fusion fashions do not yet reliably harmonize convolutional and attention fields to yield both regional and holistic 
representation10. Even after employing self-attention, the resulting feature planes often do not fully converge 
on the anisotropic textural gradients surrounding a malignancy’s margin, thereby deterring optimal early 
diagnostic fidelity. Additionally, datasets remain imbalanced along demographic, anatomic, and histological 
spectra, artificially elevating the performance of models in homogeneous validation while inhibiting true clinical 
generalizability11. As a corollary, the verification of learned features through clinical pathophysiology, especially 
multi-spectral dermoscopic sequences and histo-cytometric graphs, is still, in most scenarios, a post-hoc 
revelation, thus relegating interpretative confidence rather than embodying it in the feature extraction phase12.
Hybrid and graph-based approaches
Straightforward transformer-based methods often miss the subtleties of lesion relationships that are key to 
differential diagnosis. While integrating Vision Transformer features with Graph Neural Networks has shown 
promise in several domains, hardly any work has applied this blend specifically to the classification of skin

cancer13. Moreover, the prevailing literature emphasizes performance metrics at the expense of interpretability, 
leaving dermatologists without clear, actionable insights. We address these gaps by presenting a hybrid ViT-
GNN architecture that couples a refined attention layer with dedicated explainability modules14. Both SHAP 
and Grad-CAM are embedded into the training pipeline, and the entire framework is validated against 
multiple benchmark and clinical-derived datasets, yielding robust accuracy and transparent, clinically relevant 
visual justifications15. A compelling hybrid architecture by Koparde et al.16 uses Conditional GANs, YOLOv5 
Darknet, and Independent Component Analysis to improve skin lesion localization and categorization. The 
architecture is robust and interpretable because the cGAN module generates high-resolution, context-aware 
embeddings, YOLOv5 sharply localises them, and Independent Component Analysis (ICA) distils the salient 
features, reducing noise and enhancing contrast. These phases yield dermoscopic estimates that surpass current 
benchmarks across multiple test datasets, demonstrating the superiority of their approach.
Deep learning-based approaches for skin cancer detection
Because of their proficiency in automatic hierarchical feature extraction, Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) 
has emerged as the most prominent architecture for skin lesion classification. CNN-based models have shown 
accuracy comparable to dermatologists in melanoma detection according to Esteva et al.6 research. In any case, 
some severe disadvantages exist among CNNs which comprise.
• Constrained receptive field, resulting in a diminished comprehension of global dependencies in lesion struc -
tures.
• Absence of spatial connection modeling, hindering the compelling depiction of irregular lesion shapes.
• Sensitivity to fluctuations in imaging circumstances necessitating comprehensive data augmentation and pre -
processing.
Researchers have investigated hybrid models that combine various deep learning paradigms, including ViTs, 
GNNs, and attention-based networks, to enhance classification performance.
Vision transformers (ViTs) for medical image analysis
Originally created for NLP , Transformers have now been repurposed with the help of Vision Transformers (ViTs) 
to handle image processing. Unlike CNNs, ViTs self-claims images into patches and processes them with self-
attention a mechanism that solves long range dependence problems. Good examples are provided by Y ang et al.17 
and Xin et al.18, who had shown that Vision Transformers perform better than Convolutional Neural Networks 
in global feature representation tasks. ViTs have found usage in the Medical field for different classification tasks 
including diagnosing breast cancer, evaluating retinal diseases, and even categorizing skin lesions. But ViTs need 
larger training sets, and they often do not model spatial structures well, proving less efficient for mimicking 
detail in irregular lesion boundary precision. This study tackles those shortcomings by implementing GNNs that 
allow for better lesions spatial relation modeling.
Graph neural networks (GNNs) for skin lesion analysis
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are increasingly recognized in the field of medical image processing due to 
their capability to capture non-Euclidean spatial relationships. Unlike Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), 
which are designed for structured grid layouts, GNNs efficiently comprehend the topological interdependencies 
inherent in lesion structures by modeling images as graphs. Recent research, including work by Santoso et al.19 
and Raval et al.20, has examined GNN-based approaches for segmenting and classifying tumors. Attention 
mechanisms play a crucial role in deep learning, allowing models to focus on vital features while filtering out 
irrelevant information. A variety of attention-centric architectures have been studied within the realm of medical 
image analysis, such as Self-Attention, Channel-Spatial Attention, and Transformer-based Attention systems.
In skin cancer classification, attention processes help:
• To pay attention to diagnostically relevant lesion areas to increase classification accuracy.
• To improve feature extraction during multi-scale analysis of lesions to capture intricate features.
• To improve the interpretability of the models so that the judgments made by AI can be more easily compre -
hended by dermatologists.

Proposed methodology
In this section the authors propose a new model blending Hybrid Vision Transformer (ViT)22 and Graph Neural 
Network (GNN)23, which employs Region-Adaptive Attention (RAA)24 for skin cancer prediction. The proposed 
framework improves the accuracy and interpretability of the models by including global feature extraction, 
spatial connection modeling, and adaptive attention mechanisms. This method consists of five main parts: Data 
cleansing, vision transformer feature extraction, graph neural networking for feature spatial relations modeling, 
region adaptive attention (RAA), and multi-scale analysis of lesions with meta-learning optimization.

The proposed architecture shown in Fig.  2 consists of:
 1. Image Uploading Module - Processes dermoscopic images to enable feature variability with standardization

 2. ViT Feature Extractor - Captures global dependencies in dermatological lesions images.

 6. MLO - Adapts the model to improve performance for different skin colors and imaging conditions.

Vision transformer (ViT) feature extraction
The ViT module supplants conventional CNNs for feature extraction. ViT segments images into fixed-size patches 
and utilizes self-attention techniques to acquire feature representations rather than employing convolutional 
filters.
Procedures in ViT feature extraction26:
 (1) Image patch embedding—The input image is segmented into 16 × 16 patches, each converted into a feature 
vector.
 (2) Positional encoding—Spatial information is retained by incorporating positional encodings into the patch 
embeddings.
 (3) Multi-head self-attention (MHSA)—Calculates attention weights to identify global dependencies within 
the image.
 (4) Feedforward network (FFN)—Non-linear adjustments augment feature representations before transmit -
ting to the classifier.
In contrast to CNNs, ViTs enable the model to capture long-range relationships in skin lesion patterns, enhancing 
classification accuracy.

How RAA improves over conventional attention in lesion-focused tasks
Standard attention modules within Vision Transformers (ViTs) and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) commonly 
evaluate importance from a broad global perspective, applying similar attention weights to all image tokens and 
graph nodes without regard for the questioned clinical context in which the observation resides. This global 
uniformity introduces two notable drawbacks in the context of skin cancer detection:
• The mechanism equitably weights the features contributing to clinically ambiguous backgrounds—such as 
hair strands, benign skin texture, and photometric inconsistencies—while the decisive lesion behaviours, 
represented by irregular peripheries and subtle chromatic inhomogeneity, receive attenuated representation 
as the spurious attention influence spreads across the entire image instead of contracting compactly around 
the abnormality itself.
• Region-adaptive attention (RAA) addresses these distortions by explicitly isolating and magnifying patches in 
the vicinity of the lesion, concentrating the receptive field on a biologically relevant neighborhood and thus 
freeing computational and architectural capacity from the irrelevant periphery to the diagnostically decisive 
segments of the image.

Multi-scale lesion analysis with meta-learning optimization
Skin lesions are relatively easier to categorize when an individual phenomena is considered in comparison to the 
collection of different phenomena due to significant intraclass variability and interclass similarities. To improve 
robustness, we propose a Multi-Scale Lesion Analysis Framework that analyzes the lesion’s characteristics on 
different scale sizes.
Steps for Multi-Scale Analysis:
 (1) Obtain differences of a lesion at multiple levels - Ensures model captures coarse and fine details of other 
identified lesions.
 (2) Attention Based Feature Weighting - Combines features of the different scales, differentially for every scale.
 (3) Meta-learning For Model Rigidity - Refines model parameters on hostile datasets while ensuring adaptation 
to different skin colors and imaging conditions.
Meta-learning enables quick adaptation of model to new data distributions, improving performance on 
heterogeneous populations.
Training procedure and evaluation metrics
The adaptive learning rate stochastic gradient descent (SGD) algorithm is used to train the model. The loss 
function used during training is Focal Loss, which is often applied to rectify class imbalances in melanoma 
datasets. Performance assessment is done using:
• Precision (ACC).
• Precision, Recall, and F1 Score.
• Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC).
• Interpretability Index (for XAI assessment).
The proposed model will be compared with CNN models (ResNet, EfficientNet), Transformer models (Swin-
Transformer, DeiT), and fusion models (CNN-GNN architectures) (Fig. 2).

• Using Vision Transformers (ViTs) for global feature extraction.
• Employing Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to model spatial relationships.

Optimizing size of image patches
We carefully analyzed how the ViT patch size affects the model’s ability to acquire spatial features for melanoma 
classification, since this choice determines the spatial granularity of the input signal.
• Default Configuration (16 × 16 segments):
Adopting the standard ViT patch dimension of 16 × 16 strikes a balance between computation and spatial 
resolution. The patch encompasses the surrounding pixels necessary to convey contextual features, while still 
retaining the resolution required to resolve fine lesion details.
• Alternatives evaluated
To measure the robustness of this choice, we explored 8 × 8 and 32 × 32 patches. 8 × 8 patches (higher granularity): 
The model collects more peripheral pixels and captures richer border features, but the total token count inflates 
by approximately 2.5×, raising memory requirements and computation time. 32 × 32 patches (lower granularity): 
The total token count decreases, resulting in reduced memory usage and accelerated convergence. However, 
the loss of contextual neighbors around the lesion, combined with the upsampling of the head, renders the fine 
margin fuzzy, causing clear spatial aliasing and lowering the observed accuracy.

8 × 8 provided a modest gain in boundary localization (+ 2.9% on irregular melanoma) but incurred a 2.5× higher 
inference cost while improvement in overall accuracy remained negligible (+ 0.7% AUC). A 32 × 32 resolution 
resulted in a marked drop in composite sensitivity on the validation set (− 2.1%) due to missing border artifacts, 
leading to a class balance catastrophe in the convex and arrhythmic counts. 16 × 16 segments remained the clear 
choice, delivering statistically equivalent accuracy, the best sensitivity gain of 0.43% across 5 × 2 × 5 snapshot 
trials, and approximately 1.9 times testing speed resiliency.
We did not determine the patch size arbitrarily; instead, we assessed different configurations as shown in 
Table  4. The 16 × 16 patch size was deemed best as it maintained a good fusion between computational power 
and how well the lesion’s edges were defined. Smaller patches (8 × 8) did not add much and were expensive 
computationally.
As outlined in the methods of our GNN module, the Graph adjacency threshold (the metric used to determine 
whether any two lesion patches/nodes must be connected) has been selected and set to a value of 0.0 based on 
trial and error and validation tests.
• Step 1: Threshold candidates - We examined many values (0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8, and more) for cosine similarity 
patch embeddings.
• Step 2:- Threshold validation for each dataset: ISIC 2020, HAM10000, PH2.
• Step 3: Cross-dataset value stability: We noticed that around 0.7, the values nicely balance graph sparsity 
(avoiding over-connected graphs) and information flow (maintaining relevant lesion connections).
• Step 4: Conservation - It was demonstrated that dataset independence is effective in this case, so the adjacency 
threshold was set to 0.7, resulting in strong performance without dataset-specific fine-tuning.
The graph becomes extremely cluttered at 0.5, introducing noise from unnecessary patch connections. The graph 
at 0.8 is overly thin and restrictive to message passing. 0.7 is the sweet spot because it has the best balance of all, 
and it achieves the highest Accuracy, F1, and AUC scores, which is why it was selected for all datasets.

and 17 suggests that RAA boosts classification performance while ensuring alignment with critical lesion 
boundaries, thereby enhancing the model’s robustness and interpretability.
